# Filibuster Policy Failures by Topic

Updated Sep 30, 2026

Twenty bills backed by a Senate majority were blocked by the rule that most bills need 60 of 100 votes to reach a final vote. Each entry says what the bill would have done, how the vote went, and what has happened since. Vote counts, party breakdowns and sources are in the appendices.

## Democratic-backed bills

### D1. Dreamers: DREAM Act (2010)

- **What could have changed:** given about 1.9 million people brought to the U.S. as children a conditional legal status, and about 755,000 of them a path to permanent residence (2010 estimate).
- **Vote:** failed 55–41 in 2010, despite bipartisan support.
- **What actually changed:** no law has given this group legal status. DACA, a temporary program, is frozen by court rulings and had about 579,000 active recipients as of March 2023 (latest figure not confirmed).

### D2. Minimum wage: Minimum Wage Fairness Act (2014)

- **What could have changed:** raised the federal minimum wage above $7.25 (a 2014 version, $10.10, would have raised pay for about 16.5 million workers and lifted about 900,000 people out of poverty, while costing about 500,000 jobs).
- **Vote:** failed 54–42 in 2014. Almost entirely Democratic votes, with one Republican (Corker) voting yes.
- **What actually changed:** the federal minimum has stayed at $7.25 since 2009. About 30 states plus DC set higher minimums.

### D3. Union organizing: Employee Free Choice Act (2007)

- **What could have changed:** let workers form a union by signing cards ("card check") instead of requiring a secret-ballot election.
- **Vote:** failed 51–48 in 2007. Almost entirely Democratic votes, with one Republican (Specter) voting yes. Related bills: police and firefighter bargaining failed 55–43 in 2010, and the PRO Act passed the House 225–206 in 2021 but never reached a Senate vote.
- **What actually changed:** no federal law found. State-level changes were not researched.

### D4. Equal pay for women: Paycheck Fairness Act (2014)

- **What could have changed:** made it easier for workers to challenge pay gaps and required employers to justify them.
- **Vote:** failed 52–40 in 2014 (also 58–41 in 2010). Democratic votes only.
- **What actually changed:** no federal law. About 16 states plus DC now require employers to disclose pay ranges (counts vary by source).

### D5. Voting rights: John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act (2021)

- **What could have changed:** restored federal review of voting-rule changes in places with a history of discrimination, a protection the Supreme Court struck down in 2013.
- **Vote:** failed 50–49 in 2021. Effectively Democratic votes only.
- **What actually changed:** no federal law. Eleven states have passed their own voting rights acts, while 115 restrictive state laws have passed in 31 states since 2013.

### D6. Gun background checks: Manchin-Toomey amendment (2013)

- **What could have changed:** required background checks on most private and online gun sales.
- **Vote:** failed 54–46 in 2013. Co-written by a Republican and a Democrat.
- **What actually changed:** a 2022 law tightened checks for buyers under 21, but there is no national requirement for all sales. About 18 states plus DC require checks on all sales.

### D7. Energy efficiency: Energy Savings and Industrial Competitiveness Act (2014)

- **What could have changed:** set new energy-efficiency measures for buildings, manufacturers and federal agencies.
- **Vote:** failed 55–36 in May 2014. Nearly all Democratic votes, plus two Republicans (Ayotte and Collins); Majority Leader Reid voted no as a procedural move.
- **What actually changed:** pieces passed later. A 2015 law covers commercial-building and water-heater efficiency, and the 2021 infrastructure law funds industrial efficiency centers ($150 million) and building-code grants ($45 million a year). A data-center provision was dropped. Whether the rest of the 2014 package was ever enacted was not confirmed.

### D8. Military sexual assault: Military Justice Improvement Act (2014)

- **What could have changed:** taken the decision to prosecute sexual assault and other serious crimes away from military commanders and given it to independent military prosecutors.
- **Vote:** failed 55–45 in March 2014. Bipartisan both ways: about ten Republicans voted yes and about ten Democrats voted no.
- **What actually changed:** largely compensated for the crimes it covers. A 2021 defense law created an independent Office of Special Trial Counsel, in effect since Dec 28, 2023, for 13 serious offenses including sexual assault. Commanders keep other powers, such as choosing jury members.

### D9. Climate: American Clean Energy and Security Act (2009–10)

- **What could have changed:** capped national greenhouse gas emissions through a tradable-permit (cap-and-trade) system and set new renewable-energy standards.
- **Vote:** never brought to a Senate vote. The House passed it 219–212 in June 2009, and Senate leaders said the 60 votes were missing. Some moderate Democrats also had doubts.
- **What actually changed:** no national carbon cap. The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act, passed by simple majority, added tax incentives and grants; a 2022 model projected emissions 31–44% below 2005 levels by 2030, versus 24–35% without it. A 2025 law then ended wind and solar credits early and ended the EV credit on Sept 30, 2025, and the EPA repealed its 2009 endangerment finding on Feb 12, 2026 (court challenges pending).

### D10. Health insurance public option (2009)

- **What could have changed:** created a government-run health plan competing with private insurers in the ACA marketplaces.
- **Vote:** never voted on. It was dropped in December 2009 after Sen. Lieberman refused to support it. Supporters claimed about 51 votes, but that was never tested, so party breakdown is unknown.
- **What actually changed:** no federal plan. Three states run one: Washington (2021; about 40% of marketplace enrollees in 2026), Colorado (2023; nearly half of about 282,500 enrollees in 2025) and Nevada (2026; 10,762 enrollees against a 35,000 projection). Efforts in Maine, Minnesota and New Mexico have stalled.

### D11. Campaign spending disclosure: DISCLOSE Act (2010)

- **What could have changed:** required corporations, unions and outside groups to disclose spending on election ads.
- **Vote:** failed 57–41 (July 2010) and 59–39 (September 2010), one vote short. Democratic votes only in September: every Democrat and independent voted yes and every Republican who voted said no. A 2012 version failed 51–44 and 53–45.
- **What actually changed:** no federal law, though the bill has passed the House several times. Dark-money spending reached at least $1.9 billion in the 2024 election cycle. Some states are pursuing their own rules in 2026; a state-by-state count was not verified.

### D12. LGBTQ nondiscrimination: Equality Act (2019, 2021)

- **What could have changed:** banned discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity in employment, housing, education and public accommodations nationwide.
- **Vote:** never reached a Senate vote, chilled by the 60-vote rule. The House passed it 236–173 in 2019 (eight Republicans yes) and 224–206 in 2021 (three Republicans yes).
- **What actually changed:** no law. A 2020 Supreme Court ruling (Bostock) covers employment only. About 22 states bar housing discrimination on this basis and 21 bar it in public accommodations. Reintroduced in April 2025 and still in committee.

### D13. Contraception: Right to Contraception Act (2024)

- **What could have changed:** created a federal statutory right to get and provide contraception.
- **Vote:** failed 51–39 in June 2024. Bipartisan: Republicans Collins and Murkowski voted yes. Other Republicans called it an election-year show vote and raised concerns about abortion-related drugs and religious liberty.
- **What actually changed:** no federal right. The ACA already requires most private plans to cover contraception at no cost. Eleven states plus DC have laws protecting the right, and four states protect it in their constitutions.

### D14. IVF: Right to IVF Act (2024)

- **What could have changed:** set national protections for IVF access, required insurers to cover it and expanded fertility treatment for veterans.
- **Vote:** failed 51–44 in September 2024. Bipartisan: all Democrats plus Republicans Collins and Murkowski voted yes.
- **What actually changed:** no coverage mandate. An October 2025 administration announcement cut prices on three IVF drugs (up to $2,200 per cycle by one estimate) and clarified that employers may offer fertility benefits. State coverage laws vary widely and skip employer-run plans that cover about two-thirds of insured workers.

### D15. Health insurance subsidies: enhanced ACA premium credits (2025)

- **What could have changed:** extended the enhanced ACA premium subsidies that expired at the end of 2025.
- **Vote:** the Democratic bill failed 51–48 on Dec 9, 2025, and a Republican alternative failed by the same count. Party breakdowns were not checked.
- **What actually changed:** the credits expired Jan 1, 2026. The House passed a three-year extension 230–196 on Jan 8, 2026, but no Senate passage was found, and a bipartisan two-year compromise was under discussion. Later status was not confirmed.

### D16. January 6 Commission (2021)

- **What could have changed:** created an independent, bipartisan ten-member commission to investigate the Jan 6, 2021 attack on the Capitol.
- **Vote:** failed 54–35 on May 28, 2021 (11 senators absent), after the House passed it 252–175 with 35 Republicans. Bipartisan: six Republicans voted yes (Collins, Murkowski, Portman, Romney, Cassidy and Sasse).
- **What actually changed:** no independent commission. The House created its own Select Committee on Jul 1, 2021, which produced its own findings.

## Republican-backed bills

### R1. Abortion limits: Pain-Capable Unborn Child Protection Act (2018)

- **What could have changed:** banned most abortions nationwide after 20 weeks.
- **Vote:** failed 51–46 in 2018 (also 54–42 in 2015). Bipartisan: three Democrats voted yes and two Republicans voted no.
- **What actually changed:** no federal limit. After the Supreme Court's 2022 Dobbs ruling, 13 states ban abortion, another 6 to 7 limit it to 6–12 weeks, and 9 states plus DC set no time limit.

### R2. Immigration enforcement: Kate's Law (2016) and Stop Dangerous Sanctuary Cities Act (2016)

- **What could have changed:** penalized sanctuary cities, required prison time for people who re-enter after deportation (Kate's Law), and tightened refugee screening.
- **Vote:** failed with 53 to 55 votes in 2015–16. Kate's Law was bipartisan (55–42, three Democrats yes).
- **What actually changed:** Congress funded enforcement, about $170.7 billion in 2025 and about $70 billion more in 2026, and passed the Laken Riley Act. No law penalizes sanctuary cities or enacts Kate's Law.

### R3. Voter ID: Husted photo-ID amendment (2026)

- **What could have changed:** required photo ID to vote in federal elections nationwide.
- **Vote:** failed 53–47 in 2026. Republican votes only.
- **What actually changed:** no federal law. 36 states request or require ID, 24 of them photo ID.

### R4. Transgender athletes: Protection of Women and Girls in Sports Act (2025)

- **What could have changed:** barred transgender girls and women from girls' and women's school sports nationwide.
- **Vote:** failed 51–45 in 2025. Republican votes only.
- **What actually changed:** no federal law. In June 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that states may enact bans, and 27 states have.

## Appendix A: Democratic-backed bills, detail

### D1. Immigration: Dreamers (DREAM Act)

- **Would have been enacted:** a path to legal status for people brought to the U.S. as children. The Dec 18, 2010 cloture vote on H.R. 5281 failed 55–41; the 2007 vote on S. 2205 failed 52–44. **Bipartisan:** Republicans Bennett, Lugar and Murkowski voted yes; five Democrats (Baucus, Hagan, Nelson of NE, Pryor, Tester) voted no. The House later passed the American Dream and Promise Act in 2019 (237–187) with no Senate action.
- **Enacted anyway:** no statute. DACA (executive action, 2012) survives in part; the Fifth Circuit (Jan 17, 2025) found major parts of the DACA rule unlawful, and new initial applications are accepted but not processed. Some states grant in-state tuition or aid, but courts are rolling that back: the Fifth Circuit (Jul 9, 2026) upheld ending Texas in-state tuition, and a federal court (Jul 24, 2026) ruled Illinois's laws unlawful as applied. **Uncompensated:** permanent status and a citizenship route.

### D2. Minimum wage

- **Would have been enacted:** a higher federal minimum wage (S. 2223 cloture failed 54–42 on Apr 30, 2014; H.R. 2's $15 wage was stripped from the 2021 relief bill by the Byrd rule). **Bipartisan?** Essentially party-line: Corker was the lone Republican yes on the 2014 vote, so it counts as bipartisan only under the strict one-crossover rule. It was divided-government legislation (Republican House).
- **Enacted anyway:** a 2007 wage increase (P.L. 110-28) reached $7.25 on Jul 24, 2009, and the federal rate has not moved since. About 30 states plus DC now set higher minimums (sources count 30–31). **Uncompensated:** a federal wage above $7.25.

### D3. Union organizing (Employee Free Choice Act)

- **Would have been enacted:** card-check organizing (H.R. 800), which failed 51–48 on Jun 26, 2007 ([roll call 110-1 #227](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1101/vote_110_1_00227.htm)). **Bipartisan?** Essentially party-line: Specter was the lone Republican yes, so it counts as bipartisan only under the strict one-crossover rule. It came under a Republican president. Related: public-safety bargaining (S. 3991) failed 55–43 in 2010, and the PRO Act passed the House 225–206 in 2021 and was chilled in the Senate.
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal card-check or bargaining statute was found. State-level and court changes were not researched. **Uncompensated:** federal organizing rules.

### D4. Pay equity (Paycheck Fairness Act)

- **Would have been enacted:** stronger federal equal-pay remedies. Cloture failed 58–41 in 2010 (S. 3772), 52–47 in 2012 (S. 3220) and 52–40 in 2014 (S. 2199, 8 not voting). **Party-line:** on the 2014 roll call every yes vote was a Democrat. The House passed H.R. 7 in 2019 (242–187).
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal Paycheck Fairness Act. States filled part of the gap: pay-transparency laws in roughly 16 states plus DC as of Aug 1, 2026 (sources range 13–18), and salary-history bans in about 17 or more states. **Uncompensated:** federal coverage, uniform employer obligations and the bill's remedies.

### D5. Voting rights

- **Would have been enacted:** the John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act (S. 4, Nov 3, 2021, 50–49), which restores federal preclearance; also D.C. voting representation (S. 1257, 2007, 57–42). H.R. 1 passed the House in 2019 (234–193) and 2021 (220–210); its Senate motion to proceed tied 50–50. **Bipartisan?** Only barely: Murkowski voted yes on S. 4, and Schumer's no was procedural, so it counts as bipartisan under the strict rule but was effectively party-line.
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal restoration. The bill was reintroduced (H.R. 14, Mar 2025; S. 2523, Jul 2025) and sits in committee. Eleven states now have their own voting rights acts (CA 2002, WA 2018, OR 2019, VA 2021, NY 2022, CT 2023, MN 2024, CO 2025, MD, DE and NJ 2026). Meanwhile the Brennan Center counts 115 restrictive laws in 31 states since Shelby County. **Uncompensated:** federal preclearance and nationwide standards.

### D6. Gun background checks

- **Would have been enacted:** expanded background checks on gun sales (Manchin-Toomey, S. 649, Apr 17, 2013, 54–46, blocked by a unanimous-consent 60-vote threshold and so chilled). The House passed H.R. 8 in 2019 (240–190) and 2021 (227–203). **Bipartisan?** Manchin-Toomey was co-written by a Republican, but its party breakdown was not verified this session.
- **Enacted anyway:** the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act (P.L. 117-159, Jun 25, 2022; Senate 65–33) expanded checks for under-21 buyers, clarified who is "engaged in the business," closed the boyfriend loophole and funded extreme-risk protection orders, but did not mandate universal checks. Per Giffords, 18 states plus DC require checks on all gun sales (another Giffords page says 19), and 22 states plus DC cover unlicensed sales in some form. **Uncompensated:** a federal universal-check requirement.

### D7. Energy efficiency

- **Would have been enacted:** the Energy Savings and Industrial Competitiveness Act (S. 2262). Cloture on the motion to proceed failed 55–36 on May 7, 2014 ([roll call 113-2 #142](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1132/vote_113_2_00142.htm)); Republican yeas: Ayotte and Collins; Democratic nay: Reid (procedural). **Bipartisan under the strict rule**, but nearly party-line. The bill was divided-government legislation (Republican House).
- **Enacted anyway:** the Energy Efficiency Improvement Act of 2015 (S. 535, [P.L. 114-11](https://www.congress.gov/114/plaws/publ11/PLAW-114publ11.pdf), Apr 30, 2015), covering a Tenant Star program, water heaters and federal-building benchmarking; the House version's data-center provision was removed after a CBO estimate ([ASE](https://www.ase.org/resources/energy-efficiency-improvement-act-2015)). The 2021 infrastructure law added $150 million for industrial efficiency centers, $45 million a year for building-code grants, and $10 million each for two training programs ([Sen. Shaheen](https://www.shaheen.senate.gov/news/press/key-shaheen-portman-energy-efficiency-provisions-signed-into-law-in-bipartisan-infrastructure-legislation)).

### D8. Military sexual assault

- **Would have been enacted:** the Military Justice Improvement Act (S. 1752): independent prosecutors decide whether to try sexual assault and other serious crimes. Cloture failed 55–45 on Mar 6, 2014 ([roll call 113-2 #59](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1132/vote_113_2_00059.htm)). **Bipartisan both ways:** the roll-call summary lists ten Republican yeas (including Collins, Cruz, Grassley, Paul and Vitter) and ten Democratic nays (including Carper, Kaine, Levin, Manchin, McCaskill, Reed, Tester, Warner and Whitehouse); one machine-read summary listed more names than the tally allows, so the exact lists should be confirmed on senate.gov.
- **Enacted anyway:** the FY2022 NDAA created the Office of Special Trial Counsel, effective Dec 28, 2023, with 13 covered offenses (rape and sexual assault, child sexual offenses, intimate-image offenses, murder, manslaughter, kidnapping, stalking, domestic violence, retaliation and others), and sexual harassment added Jan 1, 2025. Commanders keep pretrial confinement, panel selection, witness immunity and post-trial review ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/criminal_justice/resources/magazine/2024-summer/military-justice-reform-current-state/)).

### D9. Climate (cap-and-trade)

- **Would have been enacted:** the American Clean Energy and Security Act (H.R. 2454), a national emissions cap-and-trade system plus renewable standards. House passage 219–212 (Jun 26, 2009). No Senate vote; Majority Leader Reid said "we know we don't have the votes" (per CAP, Dec 2019). Some moderate Democrats also opposed it, so a simple-majority outcome is uncertain. No Senate party breakdown exists.
- **Enacted anyway:** the Inflation Reduction Act (2022, reconciliation): clean-energy credits, grants and loan authority. Rhodium Group modeling put 2030 emissions 31–44% below 2005 levels, versus 24–35% without the act ([Bipartisan Policy Center summary](https://bipartisanpolicy.org/article/inflation-reduction-act-summary-energy-climate-provisions/)). The 2025 reconciliation law ended solar and wind credits for projects not started by Jul 4, 2026 or in service by Dec 31, 2027, and ended the clean vehicle credit on Sep 30, 2025 ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/congress-phases-out-energy-tax-credits)). The EPA finalized repeal of the 2009 endangerment finding on Feb 12, 2026, removing federal vehicle greenhouse-gas standards; California and others plan challenges ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/02/epa-finalizes-repeal-of-greenhouse-gas-endangerment-finding)). State carbon-pricing programs were not researched.

### D10. Public option

- **Would have been enacted:** a government-run plan in the ACA marketplaces. Never brought to a vote (Oct–Dec 2009); Sen. Harkin said "comfortably, 51 votes" existed, Sen. Baucus doubted 60, and Sen. Lieberman's opposition ended it (CAP, Dec 2019). No roll call exists, so no bipartisan or party-line flag can be verified.
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal plan. State versions: Washington's Cascade Select (2021; 40% of marketplace enrollees in 2026), Colorado Option (2023; nearly 50% of about 282,500 enrollees in 2025), Nevada's Battle Born State Plans (2026; 10,762 enrollees versus 35,000 projected). Minnesota, Maine and New Mexico efforts stalled; results are described as mixed ([Stateline, Mar 2026](https://stateline.org/2026/03/02/states-try-public-option-obamacare-plans-to-reduce-coverage-costs/)).

### D11. DISCLOSE Act

- **Would have been enacted:** S. 3628 (2010), requiring corporations, unions and outside groups to disclose election-ad spending. Cloture on the motion to proceed failed 57–41 (Jul 27, 2010) and 59–39 (Sep 23, 2010, [roll call 111-2 #240](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1112/vote_111_2_00240.htm)). **Party-line:** 57 Democrats and 2 independents voted yes; 39 Republicans voted no; Hutchison and Murkowski did not vote. The 2012 version (S. 3369) failed 51–44 and 53–45; its splits were not checked.
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal statute. Per the Brennan Center, dark-money groups spent at least $1.9 billion in the 2024 cycle and $4.3 billion since Citizens United, and the DISCLOSE Act and Honest Ads Act have passed the House multiple times without Senate action ([Brennan Center, May 2026](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/money-politics-roundup-may-2026)). It names Alaska, California, Missouri and Montana as states pursuing related measures in 2026; how many states already require disclosure was not verified.

### D12. LGBTQ nondiscrimination (Equality Act)

- **Would have been enacted:** H.R. 5, a ban on sexual-orientation and gender-identity discrimination across employment, housing, education and public accommodations. It passed the House 236–173 (May 17, 2019; eight Republicans) and 224–206 (Feb 25, 2021; Reps. Reed, Katko and Fitzpatrick). No Senate roll call was found, so no cloture tally or Senate party split exists; the House vote was bipartisan ([Wikipedia summary](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_Act_(United_States)>)).
- **Enacted anyway:** no statute. Bostock v. Clayton County (Jun 2020) reads Title VII to cover employment only. Per the Movement Advancement Project, 22 states bar housing discrimination on sexual orientation and gender identity and 21 bar it in public accommodations; the page's own summary of how many states cover all three areas was internally inconsistent, so that count is not stated ([MAP](https://mapresearch.org/equality-map/nondiscrimination-laws/)). H.R. 15 and S. 1503 were introduced Apr 2025 with 215 and 46 cosponsors.

### D13. Contraception (Right to Contraception Act)

- **Would have been enacted:** S. 4381, a federal statutory right to obtain contraception and to provide it. Cloture failed 51–39 on Jun 3, 2024. **Bipartisan:** Collins and Murkowski voted yes. Sen. Cornyn called it "a show vote" ([ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Politics/senate-democrats-put-squeeze-republicans-vote-contraceptive-access/story?id=110864013)).
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal statute. The ACA (2010) requires most private plans to cover contraception without cost sharing, and Title X funds family-planning clinics. Eleven states plus DC protect the right by statute (CO, FL, IL, MA, MN, NJ, NM, OR, RI, VT, WA) and CA, MI, OH and VT do so in their constitutions ([Guttmacher](https://www.guttmacher.org/fact-sheet/right-to-contraception-in-us)). Justice Thomas's 2022 Dobbs concurrence questioned Griswold.

### D14. IVF (Right to IVF Act)

- **Would have been enacted:** S. 4445: national IVF access protections, an insurance-coverage requirement and expanded fertility treatment for veterans. Cloture failed 51–44 on Sep 17, 2024. **Bipartisan:** all Democrats plus Collins and Murkowski voted yes ([ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Politics/senate-democrats-dare-republicans-vote-ivf-bill/story?id=113759214)).
- **Enacted anyway:** no statute and no coverage mandate. The Oct 2025 administration action set discounted prices for Gonal-F, Ovidrel and Cetrotide through TrumpRx.gov (CMS: up to $2,200 per cycle) and clarified that employers may offer standalone fertility benefits or a limited health reimbursement account capped at $2,150 ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/womens-health-policy/will-trumps-announcement-expand-access-to-ivf/)). State mandates vary and exclude self-funded plans (about two-thirds of employer-insured workers); the number of states with mandates was not verified.

### D15. Enhanced ACA premium credits

- **Would have been enacted:** S. 3385 (Democratic) to extend the enhanced credits; S. 3386 (Republican alternative) offered instead. Both failed 51–48 on Dec 9, 2025 (senate cloture filibuster table, 2026-07-03). Party splits were not checked.
- **Enacted anyway:** nothing found. The enhanced credits (created in 2021, extended by the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act) expired Jan 1, 2026. The House passed H.R. 1834 (three-year extension) 230–196 on Jan 8, 2026; a bipartisan CARE Act (two-year extension with income caps and minimum premiums) was under discussion, and about 4.8 million more people were projected to become uninsured without an extension ([ASTHO](https://www.astho.org/communications/blog/2026/aca-enhanced-premium-tax-credits-legislative-developments-2025-2026/)). Later Senate action was not found in searches.

### D16. January 6 Commission

- **Would have been enacted:** H.R. 3233, an independent ten-member commission modeled on the 9/11 Commission. Cloture failed 54–35 on May 28, 2021. **Bipartisan:** six Republicans voted yes (Murkowski, Portman, Romney, Cassidy, Collins, Sasse). The House had passed it 252–175 on May 19, 2021 with 35 Republicans ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_6_commission)).
- **Enacted anyway:** no independent commission. The House formed a Select Committee on Jul 1, 2021, which issued its own findings.

## Appendix B: Republican-backed bills, detail

### R1. Abortion limits (20-week ban, born-alive, Hyde codification)

- **Would have been enacted:** a nationwide 20-week ban. Cloture failed 54–42 in 2015 (H.R. 36), 51–46 on Jan 29, 2018 (S. 2311) and 53–44 in 2020 (S. 3275); born-alive bills failed 53–44 and 56–41 (2019, 2020) and 52–47 (2025); Hyde codification (S. 109, 2019) failed 48–47. **Bipartisan?** The 2018 vote was bipartisan both ways: Democrats Casey, Donnelly and Manchin voted yes, and Republicans Collins and Murkowski voted no. The 2015, 2020 and born-alive splits were not verified.
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal statute. Dobbs overruled Roe, and states did the rest: 13 states ban abortion, another 6–7 limit it at 6–12 weeks (KFF, 2026), while 9 states plus DC set no gestational limit. **Uncompensated:** a uniform national standard; some states are more restrictive than 20 weeks and others impose none.

### R2. Immigration enforcement (sanctuary cities, Kate's Law, refugee vetting)

- **Would have been enacted:** penalties on sanctuary jurisdictions (S. 2146, Oct 2015, 54–45; S. 3100, 2016, 53–44), mandatory minimums for reentry after deportation (Kate's Law, S. 2193, Jul 6, 2016, 55–42), and tighter refugee vetting (H.R. 4038, 2016, 55–43). **Bipartisan?** Kate's Law was: Democrats Donnelly, Heitkamp and Manchin voted yes and no Republican voted no. The sanctuary-bill splits were not verified.
- **Enacted anyway:** money and detention capacity, mostly through filibuster-proof reconciliation: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Jul 4, 2025; 51–50) directs about $170.7B, and the Secure America Act (June 2026; House 214–212) adds roughly $70B. The Laken Riley Act (P.L. 119-1) passed with 10 Democrats (cloture 61–35; Senate 64–35). No enactment was found for sanctuary penalties or Kate's Law. **Uncompensated:** those policy provisions, which reconciliation cannot carry.

### R3. Voter ID and election rules

- **Would have been enacted:** a nationwide photo-ID requirement. On Mar 26, 2026 the Husted photo-ID amendment (4732) to S. 1383 got 53–47, short of 60. **Party-line:** all yeas were Republicans and all nays Democrats or independents. The SAVE America Act passed the House in February 2026 and stalled in the Senate, which voted five times on it or its elements without reaching 60; Thune says the votes are not there to change the filibuster. Not passed as of Aug 7, 2026; later status not confirmed.
- **Enacted anyway:** state laws. NCSL counts 36 states that request or require ID, 24 of them photo ID, and a federal judge upheld North Carolina's photo-ID law (Mar 2026). **Uncompensated:** a uniform national requirement and any proof-of-citizenship mandate.

### R4. Transgender athletes in girls' and women's sports

- **Would have been enacted:** a nationwide restriction (S. 9, Mar 3, 2025, cloture failed 51–45). **Party-line:** all 51 yeas were Republicans, including Collins and Murkowski; every Democrat and independent voted no; Capito and Lummis did not vote.
- **Enacted anyway:** no federal statute. On Jun 30, 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 (Kavanaugh) in West Virginia v. B.P.J. and Little v. Hecox that Idaho's and West Virginia's laws are permitted; states may ban but are not required to. 27 states have such laws. Effects of NCAA policy and executive orders were not verified. **Uncompensated:** a nationwide rule; states without laws remain free not to adopt one.
